# A2 – Truss Stress Analysis

## Objective
Design a lightweight planar truss and connecting pins using standard material properties to safely support a specified load. I selected a maximum force of $25\text{ kN}$ applied at loading points, with Point A configured as a pin support and Point B as a roller support ($a = 0.4\text{ m}$, $b = 0.3\text{ m}$).

---

## Analyze

### 1. External Reactions & Internal Static Analysis
I started by creating quick hand sketches of potential truss geometries to connect all required nodes while minimizing structural weight. I selected a triangulated 5-member configuration (AB, AC, AD, BC, CD) because triangular units efficiently distribute large applied forces. I am aware that adding an extra pin in between A and B and creating 3 triangles would have created a more even distribution. However, application of the truss was not given and I wanted to create a simple truss that would be less time consuming to solve for.

Next, I solved for the external reaction forces by taking a moment equilibrium equation about Pin A ($\Sigma M_A = 0$), which allowed me to isolate and calculate the vertical reaction at Roller B ($B_y$). With the support reactions established, I determined internal member forces using the Method of Joints:

* **Joint B:** Summed forces in the vertical direction ($\Sigma F_y = 0$) to set the roller reaction equal to the vertical component of Member BC, yielding the force magnitude for BC. Summing forces horizontally ($\Sigma F_x = 0$) gave the tension force in Member AB.
* **Joint C & Joint D:** Calculated the member angles using trigonometry to resolve force components. I summed forces vertically and horizontally across Joints C and D, accounting for the applied $25\text{ kN}$ downward loads at each node.
* **Maximum Force:** Comparing all internal member results revealed that Member AB experiences the largest internal force of **$87.58\text{ kN}$ in compression**.

### 2. Member Cross-Sectional Area & Frame Weight
Using the maximum compressive load ($P_{\text{max}} = 87.58\text{ kN}$), I calculated the required uniform cross-sectional area for all members:

* Applied a safety factor of $3.5$ against the yield strength of A500 structural steel ($\sigma_y \approx 317\text{ MPa}$).
* Symbolically solved for minimum cross-sectional area:

$$A_{\text{min}} = \frac{P_{\text{max}} \cdot FS}{\sigma_y}$$

* Numerically evaluated the required area:

$$A_{\text{min}} = \frac{(87,580\text{ N}) \times 3.5}{3.17 \times 10^8\text{ N/m}^2} = \mathbf{967\text{ mm}^2} \quad (9.67 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2)$$

To determine total frame weight, I summed the total member lengths ($L_{\text{total}} = 3.454\text{ m}$ across members AB, AC, AD, BC, CD), multiplied by $A_{\text{min}}$ to find the total steel volume, and applied the density of steel ($\rho = 7850\text{ kg/m}^3$):

$$\text{Weight}_{\text{truss}} = A_{\text{min}} \cdot L_{\text{total}} \cdot \rho \cdot g = \mathbf{257.2\text{ N}} \quad (26.22\text{ kg})$$

---

### 3. Single-Shear Pin Sizing & Pin Weight
Next, I sized the connecting pins located at joints A, B, C, and D:

* **Material & Safety Factor:** Hardened tool steel with a yield shear strength $\tau_y = 170\text{ ksi}$ ($1.1721 \times 10^9\text{ N/m}^2$), density $\rho_{\text{pin}} = 7694\text{ kg/m}^3$, and a safety factor of $4.0$ under single shear ($n = 1$).
* **Symbolic Pin Area:**

$$\tau_{\text{allow}} = \frac{\tau_y}{FS} = \frac{V_{\text{max}}}{A_{\text{pin}}} \implies A_{\text{pin}} = \frac{V_{\text{max}} \cdot FS}{\tau_y}$$

* **Numerical Pin Area:**

$$A_{\text{pin}} = \frac{(87,580\text{ N}) \times 4.0}{1.1721 \times 10^9\text{ N/m}^2} = \mathbf{298.9\text{ mm}^2} \quad (\text{Radius } r = 9.755\text{ mm}, \text{ Diameter } d = 19.51\text{ mm})$$

For a square member profile ($31.1\text{ mm} \times 31.1\text{ mm}$ cross-section giving area $967\text{ mm}^2$), the pin length spanning a single-shear joint between two overlapping members is $L_{\text{pin}} = 2 \times 31.1\text{ mm} = 62.2\text{ mm}$ ($0.0622\text{ m}$). Calculating the combined volume for all 4 pins gave a total pin weight of:

$$\text{Weight}_{\text{pins}} = 4 \cdot A_{\text{pin}} \cdot L_{\text{pin}} \cdot \rho_{\text{pin}} \cdot g = \mathbf{5.61\text{ N}} \quad (0.572\text{ kg})$$

---

### 4. CAD Modeling & Weight Verification
After completing hand calculations, I modeled the 3D geometry in CAD:

* Extruded the truss frame as a single part, maintaining a constant member cross-section of $967\text{ mm}^2$ ($31.1\text{ mm} \times 31.1\text{ mm}$ square profile).
* Modeled the 4 joint pins as cylindrical parts with a diameter of $19.51\text{ mm}$ ($A_{\text{pin}} = 298.9\text{ mm}^2$) and length of $62.2\text{ mm}$.
* Assigned material mass densities to both parts in CAD and evaluated software Mass Properties.

The CAD model mass properties closely matched the hand calculations ($257.2\text{ N}$ for the truss frame and $5.61\text{ N}$ for the pins). Small variances are due to joint node overlaps where member ends meet at pin locations.

---

## Decide
I selected a 5-member planar triangulated geometry because it minimizes total member count and overall structural weight while maintaining high rigidity under vertical loads. Choosing a square profile ($31.1\text{ mm} \times 31.1\text{ mm}$) provided a uniform extrusion thickness that cleanly accommodated single-shear pin connections at each joint node.

---

## Communicate

### Engineering Lessons Learned
* **Yield Strength vs. External Stress Sizing:** I learned how to size structural elements by directly comparing allowable internal stress against material yield strength modified by safety factors, ensuring structural integrity without adding unnecessary material weight.
* **Joint Thickness & Pin Weight Interaction:** I discovered that pin length depends directly on beam cross-sectional geometry in single-shear joints, meaning thicker member profiles increase total pin engagement length and directly add hardware weight.
* **Hand Calculation vs. CAD Verification:** I realized the importance of computing physical mass properties by hand prior to modeling in CAD, allowing me to verify material density assignments and catch dimensional errors during software evaluation.
